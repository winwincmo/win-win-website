If you're running a growing B2B company, there's a good chance your marketing needs have become more complicated over the past few years. You may have someone managing the website, an agency running advertising, and a salesperson who handles a surprising amount of marketing communication because nobody else has the time.

At some point, the question becomes whether it's time to hire a marketing leader or build a bigger team. It's a reasonable question, but I think AI gives us an opportunity to reconsider the way we answer it.

For years, companies have built marketing teams by identifying the capabilities they need and hiring people to provide them. That approach still makes sense in many situations. But AI-supported workflows now offer alternatives that can reduce the amount of permanent staffing needed for some kinds of execution.

The opportunity is to build a marketing function around the needs of the business, with experienced leadership directing the work and technology extending the team's capacity. Here's how I'd approach it.

## Start with the way your company wins business

Before deciding who to hire, I'd want to understand where your revenue comes from and how customers make purchasing decisions.

For many established B2B businesses, the sales process revolves around relationships developed over time. A customer might be introduced through a referral partner, or someone on your sales team may have spent years building the relationship before an opportunity appears. Your marketing investment should strengthen that process.

Look at where your best opportunities originate and what happens before they become customers. Ask sales leadership where buyers get stuck and which questions come up repeatedly. You may discover that your biggest marketing opportunity has very little to do with generating more leads.

Perhaps prospects arrive at your website after a referral but struggle to understand what makes your company different. That suggests a positioning or messaging problem worth addressing. Or maybe sales is generating plenty of opportunities, but the team lacks convincing materials to communicate the value of a complex service. In that case, stronger content may be more valuable than another advertising campaign.

The point is to understand the business opportunity before deciding which marketing capabilities to invest in. If you want to explore that idea further, I wrote about it in [Is There a Marketing Formula That Works for Relationship-Based Sales?](/blog/marketing-formula-relationship-based-sales/).

## Decide which decisions require senior leadership

One of the most common staffing mistakes I see is hiring for execution before establishing who will make the strategic decisions.

A capable marketing specialist can do excellent work, but someone needs to determine what the work should accomplish and how it fits into the broader business. That leadership responsibility includes deciding where to invest the marketing budget and which initiatives deserve priority. It also involves working with sales leadership to understand what's happening with customers.

For a smaller business, the CEO may currently be making those decisions. As the organization grows, that becomes difficult to sustain alongside everything else the CEO is responsible for. That's when experienced marketing leadership becomes valuable.

The leader doesn't necessarily need to be full-time. A fractional CMO may be able to participate in the executive conversations that matter and direct the marketing program according to an agreed level of involvement.

The important distinction is between needing more people to produce marketing and needing someone with the experience to decide what marketing should do. I've explored that decision in [Do You Need a Fractional CMO or a Bigger Marketing Team?](/blog/fractional-cmo-or-marketing-team/).

## Build around capabilities instead of job titles

Once the priorities are clear, I'd look at what the business needs to execute the plan.

Let's say you have an established B2B company that's trying to improve its reputation in a specialized market. Its website needs better content, and the sales team needs stronger materials. The company also wants to remain visible to existing customers and referral partners.

Traditionally, you might consider hiring a content specialist and a digital marketer, then adding outside support for web development. Depending on the volume of work, that could still be appropriate.

But I'd begin by looking at the capabilities required and how frequently they're needed. Do you need a web developer working full-time, or do you need reliable website support when changes are required? Does your content program justify a permanent hire, or could an experienced writer work with a well-designed AI drafting process?

Some capabilities are needed every day. Others are needed for a particular initiative or only a few hours a month. A lean marketing model makes those distinctions before adding permanent costs.

## Create a shared foundation for marketing execution

This is where the design of your marketing system becomes particularly important.

If your website content, advertising, email communications, and sales materials are all being developed independently, you're likely to encounter inconsistencies. Even talented specialists can interpret the company's positioning differently when they're working from separate briefs and information.

I'd want a shared foundation that everyone can work from. AI makes it much more practical to develop the governing assets that support that foundation. For example, we can build detailed buyer personas informed by real customer and sales conversations, rather than leaving each channel specialist to imagine the audience independently. We can conduct citation audits to examine which sources AI search and answer systems use when describing the company or its category, and where that information may be incomplete or misleading. Messaging grids can then connect the questions different buyers ask with the evidence and language we want to carry through their experience.

These assets used to require substantial effort to develop and maintain. AI can accelerate the research and organizing work, while experienced people check the sources and approve the conclusions. Once validated, the assets become a common reference for people and AI workflows alike. The website team and the person preparing sales materials no longer have to interpret the company's positioning independently. They can draw on the same understanding of the buyer, even when their deliverables look different.

I think that's a significant advantage of a connected system over a group of specialists working in silos. A well-managed shared foundation can create greater consistency across touchpoints, especially as the volume of execution grows. It still needs ownership: a persona can be based on weak assumptions, and a citation audit can miss sources or misread what an AI system is actually saying. The goal is to make the team's collective understanding more useful and repeatable, with a systems thinker responsible for keeping it accurate.

Consider a new service launch. A connected approach allows the website explanation and sales materials to draw from the same approved positioning. When the company prepares follow-up communications, those messages can reflect the questions customers are actually asking.

This doesn't mean every channel should sound identical. It means buyers encounter a consistent understanding of the company wherever they engage with it. You can see how we approach connected marketing across channels on the [Win-Win Services page](/services/).

## Identify where AI workflows can make execution more efficient

Once the foundation is in place, I'd look for work that's repeatable and consumes a meaningful amount of time.

Content preparation is an obvious place to start. AI can support research and create drafts based on approved messaging, while an experienced person evaluates whether the material is accurate and useful to the audience.

Campaign coordination is another opportunity. Depending on the tools available, workflows can organize information and prepare materials for review. They may also reduce the manual effort involved in reporting.

I'd be particularly interested in the opportunities to connect information that currently lives in separate systems. Imagine being able to bring together CRM information and campaign performance to identify patterns in buyer engagement. Or being able to recognize that certain customer questions are appearing regularly in sales conversations and need to be addressed through marketing content.

The objective is to use AI to extend the capacity of the team and improve its understanding of the buyer relationship. That requires appropriate data access and reliable information. AI-generated findings also need human evaluation, particularly when they inform spending decisions or customer-facing claims.

Our article [Can You Trust AI Agents With Your B2B Marketing?](/blog/trusting-ai-agents-b2b-marketing/) explores these considerations in more detail. [McKinsey's State of AI research](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025) also provides useful context on the importance of redesigning workflows rather than simply adding AI to existing tasks.

## Keep a systems thinker responsible for the whole picture

One thing I would be careful about is recreating the old channel silos inside an AI-powered marketing operation. It's entirely possible to build a collection of automated workflows that produce content and campaign activity without any meaningful coordination between them.

That's why the marketing leader needs to understand how the whole system fits together. Someone should be looking at the buyer experience across touchpoints and asking whether the work is reinforcing the relationships that matter to the business. That person needs to recognize when a change in positioning should influence the website or when feedback from sales suggests that a campaign needs to be reconsidered.

Human oversight also matters for quality. AI workflows may produce polished materials that contain inaccurate claims or misunderstand the intended audience. The [NIST Generative AI Risk Management Profile](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence) provides useful guidance on the importance of evaluation and oversight.

For many growing B2B businesses, this is where an experienced fractional CMO can provide value. The CMO brings a connected perspective to the work and makes decisions about how the marketing resources should operate together.

## Measure whether marketing is moving the business forward

I would establish performance measures that reflect the business's sales process rather than rely exclusively on activity reports.

If marketing is intended to improve conversion, I want to understand whether prospects are moving through the sales process more effectively. If the objective is to become better known within a particular industry, we need to look for evidence that the company is reaching and influencing the right buyers.

And if we're creating materials for the sales team, I'd want to know whether they're using them and whether those materials are improving conversations. The specific measures will depend on the business, but the principle is consistent: we direct the marketing investment around the highest priorities to move sales.

AI can support this work by organizing information and identifying patterns, but the marketing leader needs to interpret those findings in the context of the business.

## Review the marketing budget as the business evolves

A lean marketing model should give a business flexibility in how it invests.

You may need substantial outside resources during a website redevelopment or a new market launch. Once that work is complete, the ongoing requirements may be considerably smaller. Similarly, a business might decide to bring a particular capability in-house when the volume of work becomes consistent enough to justify a full-time position.

I'd revisit those decisions regularly rather than assume the current staffing model needs to remain in place indefinitely. The goal is to maintain access to the right expertise without carrying unnecessary permanent costs, which requires discipline from marketing leadership and a willingness to recommend changes in the way the work gets done.

## What might this look like in practice?

Imagine a growing professional services company with an established sales organization and a strong referral network. The business wants to strengthen its presence in two important markets but isn't ready to build a full internal marketing department.

A lean model might include a fractional CMO who works with executive leadership to establish priorities and oversee the marketing investment. The company could retain an experienced content resource to develop its most important materials while relying on specialized partners for website and campaign needs.

AI-supported workflows could assist with research and content preparation, allowing the team to accomplish more without requiring the same level of manual production effort. The company might already have an internal marketing coordinator who manages day-to-day communication and works closely with the sales team. That person remains an important part of the operation, while the fractional CMO provides direction and coordinates the broader resources.

The exact structure would depend on the company's needs. I wouldn't assume every business should use the same combination of people or technology. What matters is that the marketing function has experienced leadership and a clear connection to the work that drives revenue.

## The opportunity for growing B2B companies

I think AI gives business leaders a chance to ask a better question about marketing staffing.

Instead of starting with how many people a marketing department should have, start with what the business needs marketing to accomplish. Then consider which capabilities need to be available regularly and where more flexible resources make sense.

Experienced leadership can provide the direction, while specialized people and AI-supported workflows give the business the capacity to execute. For companies that have grown through sales relationships and referrals, this can be an especially practical approach.
