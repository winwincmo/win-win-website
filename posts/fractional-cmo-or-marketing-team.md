There's a familiar moment in the growth of a business when marketing starts to feel like something that needs more attention, more people, or more money. The sales team wants better materials, the website needs updating, campaigns aren't producing the opportunities everyone hoped for, and there are another dozen ideas competing for the marketing budget.

Eventually, someone suggests hiring another marketing person.

It's a reasonable instinct, especially when everyone is stretched thin. But before adding someone to the payroll, it's worth examining whether the business actually needs more marketing capacity or whether the people and resources already in place need stronger direction.

I've worked with businesses that have capable marketing employees, outside agencies, and meaningful marketing budgets, yet still struggle to connect all that activity to revenue. Often, the individual pieces are functioning well enough. What's missing is an experienced leader who can see how those pieces fit together, establish priorities, and make sure marketing is supporting the way the business actually wins customers.

That's where fractional marketing leadership becomes an interesting option.

## What does a fractional CMO actually do?

A fractional Chief Marketing Officer provides executive-level marketing leadership without requiring a full-time executive hire. The arrangement is typically structured around a defined scope of responsibility and a recurring commitment of time, giving the business an experienced leader who participates in important decisions and remains accountable for the direction of marketing.

The work begins with understanding the business itself, particularly how customers are acquired, how sales relationships develop, and where marketing can contribute to better outcomes.

For a company that grows through referrals, trade relationships, industry events, and direct sales, a traditional demand-generation playbook may have limited relevance. A good fractional CMO recognizes those distinctions and develops marketing around the sales motion that already exists, rather than introducing activity simply because it's common practice.

## How do you know whether to hire a fractional CMO or another marketing employee?

I would start by looking at the decisions being made.

Does the business have a documented marketing strategy that leadership has reviewed and approved? Is there a clear understanding of which customers represent the greatest opportunity? Do marketing investments have a defined purpose, and is someone regularly evaluating whether those investments are producing the intended results?

If the answers are yes, and the primary challenge is getting the work completed, another marketing employee or specialized resource may be exactly what's needed.

But if marketing priorities are changing from week to week, employees are taking direction from multiple executives, or the company is investing in campaigns without a clear connection to its sales objectives, adding another person can create more work to coordinate.

In those circumstances, experienced marketing leadership may be the more valuable first investment.

## What happens when you hire a fractional CMO?

At Win-Win, I believe the relationship should begin with a period of due diligence. Before recommending new campaigns, technology, or additional staff, we need to understand what the business has already built and how well it's functioning.

That includes reviewing the existing marketing strategy, evaluating performance, understanding the capabilities of the current team, and looking closely at how marketing supports sales. The purpose is to establish a credible picture of where the business stands and what deserves attention.

From there, we strengthen the marketing strategy and develop any foundational assets that are missing. Depending on the organization, that might involve clarifying positioning, defining priority audiences, improving messaging, or developing a more coherent marketing roadmap.

I consider CEO approval an essential part of this process. Marketing is an investment in business growth, and the person ultimately accountable for the business should understand and support the strategic direction before significant resources are committed.

We also establish an annual marketing budget that reflects the company's priorities and financial realities. That budget accounts for expenses such as advertising, trade shows, event marketing, sales materials, signage, and other activities that fall outside the fractional CMO fee.

Once the foundation is in place, the relationship moves into an operating rhythm with weekly check-ins and quarterly strategic reviews. This gives leadership regular visibility into what's happening, what decisions need to be made, and how the marketing program is performing.

## What about AI? Can it reduce the need for a larger marketing team?

This is where the economics of marketing are becoming particularly interesting.

For years, businesses have faced a fairly direct relationship between the amount of marketing work they wanted to accomplish and the number of people required to do it. More campaigns, more content, and more reporting generally meant hiring employees or engaging outside vendors.

AI is changing that equation, particularly for businesses that can organize recurring work into specialized workflows.

Research, content development, campaign preparation, and reporting can all benefit from AI-supported execution, although the quality of the output still depends on the direction being given and the oversight applied.

That creates an opportunity for businesses to combine experienced marketing leadership with a more flexible execution model. Rather than building a large permanent department before the business needs one, they can make more deliberate decisions about which capabilities should be handled internally and where specialized support makes sense.

The fractional CMO remains accountable for the strategy, priorities, and quality of the work.

## How should a business evaluate the cost of a fractional CMO?

I think it's important to evaluate marketing leadership separately from the money invested in marketing programs.

The fractional CMO fee covers the agreed leadership responsibilities and services, while advertising, trade shows, events, production, and other marketing activities require their own budgets. That distinction makes it easier for the business to understand what it's spending and why.

The financial appeal of fractional leadership is that a company can gain access to executive experience without immediately taking on the compensation and employment costs of a full-time CMO. The value, however, should ultimately be measured by the quality of decisions being made and the effectiveness of the marketing investments those decisions guide.

For a growing business, the goal is to build a marketing function that makes economic sense for its current stage while creating the capacity to support future growth.

## So, do you need a fractional CMO?

Perhaps the most useful place to begin is by asking whether your business needs more marketing work completed or whether it needs someone with the experience and authority to determine which work matters.

Sometimes the answer will be another employee. Sometimes it will be a stronger agency partner or a specialized contractor. And sometimes the business has reached the point where marketing requires executive leadership, even though a full-time CMO isn't yet the right fit.
