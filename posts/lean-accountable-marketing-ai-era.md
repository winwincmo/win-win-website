I've spent more than 25 years leading marketing teams, and I've always preferred working with a small group of talented people who are curious about the business and genuinely enjoy figuring things out together.

Some of the most valuable hours I've spent in marketing have involved people sitting around a table, working through a business challenge and coming away with a new understanding of what needs to happen next. I love the energy of those conversations, especially when people bring different perspectives and enjoy learning from one another.

Now that AI is making it possible for smaller teams to accomplish considerably more, we have an opportunity to rethink how marketing teams are organized and what we need from the people who lead them.

## How an hour is spent matters

Strategic thinkers and tactical thinkers tend to have different conversations, and they spend their time differently. Now that AI can handle a growing share of tactical execution, we need to take a closer look at how the people on our marketing teams think.

Consider how someone on your marketing team spends an hour. Are they focused on completing tasks that AI could handle, or are they curious about what the business needs next and where their thinking could make the greatest impact?

A strategic thinker knows how to find the right information and interpret what it means for the business. They can then determine what needs to happen and direct AI-supported workflows to carry out the work.

The value of that hour comes from what their thinking makes possible and what the business accomplishes as a result.

Specialized expertise still matters, particularly when a company needs technical or creative capabilities that AI cannot reliably provide. But as more production work becomes automated, the people who can use information to make sound decisions and turn those decisions into action become increasingly valuable.

## Marketing needs to understand the whole buyer relationship

Marketing departments are often organized around individual functions. That structure can make it easier to manage production, but it doesn't necessarily reflect how customers experience a company.

Think about the way a relationship might develop in a B2B business.

A prospective buyer hears about your company from a trusted colleague and asks ChatGPT how you compare with other providers. They visit your website to see whether the recommendation holds up and discover an article that answers a question they've been considering.

Months later, they meet someone from your sales team at an industry event. By then, they've already formed impressions about your expertise and whether your company might be a good fit.

From the buyer's perspective, this is one developing relationship with your brand. They aren't thinking about which marketing function produced each interaction.

That's the connective tissue I want a marketing team to understand.

Our website should reinforce what salespeople are saying in customer conversations. Our content should address the questions buyers are asking as they evaluate their options. Increasingly, we also need to understand what AI assistants are telling people who research our companies.

We can't control every AI-generated answer, but we can make our expertise easier to discover by publishing useful information that reflects what the business knows and does well.

This matters in relationship-driven B2B companies, where a purchasing decision may unfold over months or even years. [Research from LinkedIn's B2B Institute on the 95-5 rule](https://business.linkedin.com/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/trends/95-5-rule) reinforces the importance of building familiarity with buyers long before they're ready to purchase.

When marketing understands the broader buyer relationship, it can coordinate its work around what customers need instead of treating each interaction as an independent activity.

## AI gives strategic teams a much greater capacity to execute

One of the traditional limitations of lean marketing teams has been execution capacity. A small group might understand the business exceptionally well and develop a strong strategy, but delivering the work still required substantial time and specialized resources.

AI changes what's possible.

Imagine a connected marketing operation that draws from the company's approved positioning and has access to relevant customer information. Instead of keeping that knowledge in separate systems, AI agents can use a shared foundation to coordinate work.

I think of it almost as giving the marketing operation a shared brain.

Consider a marketing leader who notices that opportunities are stalling at a particular stage in the sales process. With appropriate access to the data, an AI-supported system could investigate patterns in those opportunities and identify concerns that appear to be influencing buyers.

The leader evaluates the findings and decides how the company should respond. From there, AI workflows can prepare revised messaging or develop materials that address those concerns.

The team can move from insight to execution without handing the work through several disconnected functions.

This is where I see an important difference between employees using AI individually to work faster and an organization building an integrated AI operation.

Individual AI tools can improve personal productivity, but an integrated system has the potential to retain shared business context and coordinate execution across functions. It can also preserve information from earlier work so future decisions benefit from what the organization has learned.

[McKinsey's 2025 State of AI research](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025) examines how organizations are redesigning workflows to capture more value from AI. I expect this distinction to become increasingly important in 2027 as businesses explore more coordinated agentic operations.

For lean marketing teams, this creates the possibility of substantially expanding execution capacity without building a traditional department around every marketing channel.

## Human leadership becomes more important as execution changes

An AI-powered operation still needs experienced people who understand the business and can exercise sound judgment.

A strategic leader needs to recognize when the available data isn't telling the whole story. They also need enough understanding of the customer to question recommendations that seem reasonable on paper but don't reflect what's happening in the market.

I've found that valuable marketing decisions often begin with curiosity about something that doesn't quite make sense.

Why are we attracting interest from a particular customer segment but struggling to convert it? Has something changed in the way buyers evaluate our services?

AI can make it easier to investigate these questions by bringing together information that would otherwise take considerable effort to collect and analyze. The leader can use those findings to determine what action makes sense and direct the system accordingly.

Sometimes that judgment means deciding that a task doesn't need to be done.

Just because an AI workflow can produce twenty articles doesn't mean the business needs twenty articles. The decision should come from an understanding of what buyers need and what the company is trying to accomplish.

There are also important responsibilities around accuracy and oversight. The [National Institute of Standards and Technology's guidance on generative AI risk management](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence) addresses the need to evaluate AI systems and manage the risks associated with their use.

A marketing leader needs to establish appropriate safeguards and remain accountable for the work the organization puts into the market.

As AI takes on more execution, experienced leaders have an opportunity to spend more of their time directing work that creates value.

## Accountability should reflect business impact

Marketing teams have become very good at reporting what they produce. Our platforms make it easy to count campaigns and measure engagement.

Those metrics can provide useful information, but I want to understand whether our marketing investment is contributing to the way the business grows.

For a relationship-driven B2B company, we might see that contribution in the quality of opportunities entering the sales pipeline. We might also discover through customer conversations that our marketing has addressed concerns earlier in the buying process.

The measures should reflect how the company wins business.

I explored this idea in more detail in [Is There a Marketing Formula That Works for Relationship-Based Sales?](/blog/marketing-formula-relationship-based-sales/).

An integrated AI-supported operation offers the possibility of connecting marketing activity more directly to business outcomes. When information flows between systems, we can develop a clearer understanding of how buyers engage with the company and which activities contribute to progress.

That information should influence what we do next.

Accountability also means paying attention to cost. A lean marketing operation supported by AI can reduce the labor required for some activities, but efficiency matters only when those resources are directed toward work that contributes to business results.

A lower cost per deliverable is useful. A better return on the overall marketing investment is the larger goal.

## Building a different kind of marketing team

When I founded Win-Win, I wanted to build a business around the approach to marketing leadership I've developed throughout my career.

Growing B2B companies need experienced people who understand their customers and know how to connect marketing investment to business growth. They also need a practical way to execute that strategy without necessarily hiring a full-time CMO or building an extensive internal marketing department.

AI-supported operations make that model increasingly practical.

I envision a small group of capable, curious people who understand the business deeply and enjoy working together to make it more successful. They know how to interpret what they're learning and can direct AI-powered workflows that turn their decisions into coordinated marketing activity.

This creates an opportunity to build teams around the quality of their judgment and their ability to direct execution, with greater flexibility in how the work gets accomplished.

For growing businesses, that could mean access to more experienced marketing leadership without the overhead of a large department.

And for those of us who love the creative and strategic work of marketing, it's an opportunity to spend more time doing the work where our experience makes a meaningful difference.

**AI gives us the ability to extend the reach of good strategic thinking. The opportunity now is to build marketing operations that put that thinking to work.**
