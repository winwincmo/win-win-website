I've spent more than 25 years leading marketing teams, and I've always had a preference for keeping them lean. I love working with a small group of talented people who are curious about the business and comfortable figuring things out together. They're people who can think strategically and then roll up their sleeves to make something happen.

My philosophy has been pretty consistent throughout my career. I want to put serious thinking into the strategy and then find the most efficient way to deliver the work. I've found that the size of a team has surprisingly little to do with the value it creates.

Some of the most productive hours I've spent in marketing have involved a handful of people sitting around a table trying to solve a business problem. As AI changes what's possible for a relatively small team, I think there's an opportunity to reconsider how we've traditionally organized marketing.

## An hour isn't always an hour

Let me paint a picture of the kind of team I love working with.

Imagine five people sitting around a table discussing why the business isn't gaining traction in a promising new market. Someone questions whether we're targeting the right buyers. Another person brings up something they heard in a recent customer conversation, and suddenly we realize that the messaging on the website doesn't reflect what the sales team is saying.

The conversation is lively because everyone understands enough about the business to contribute beyond their immediate area of expertise. They're comfortable challenging each other's thinking, and nobody feels the need to defend an idea simply because it was their idea.

By the end of the hour, we've developed a clearer understanding of the problem and identified a practical way forward. People are already thinking about how to make the necessary changes, and the energy in the room is contagious. I come away from those conversations feeling like we've accomplished something meaningful.

Now imagine a different kind of marketing organization. It's larger, with people assigned to specific functions. The content team is responsible for producing content. The advertising team manages campaigns, while website updates move through a development queue. Project managers keep track of assignments and make sure deliverables are completed on schedule.

I've worked with teams like this, too, and I've found those environments much harder to lead, especially when the culture places a high value on certainty. People want clearly defined instructions before they begin. They're comfortable executing an established plan but less inclined to question whether it's the right plan.

I'm not suggesting that tactical specialists lack talent or that large organizations don't need structure. Some of the most capable people I've worked with have been specialists, and there are businesses whose complexity absolutely justifies substantial marketing teams. But I've noticed that an hour spent by these two types of teams can produce entirely different kinds of value.

One team might spend that hour identifying a problem that changes the direction of the marketing program, while the other spends it producing the next round of deliverables. Both groups have worked hard, but their contributions to the business are fundamentally different. That's why I've always been more interested in the quality of the thinking than the volume of production.

## The problem with organizing marketing around channels

There's another challenge I've encountered with heavily specialized marketing teams. When people are responsible for individual channels, they can lose sight of the connections between them.

Your website specialist is thinking about website performance, while your advertising team is focused on campaign results. The content team has an editorial calendar to maintain. Each person may be doing excellent work according to the objectives they've been given, but who's looking at the relationship the buyer is developing with the company?

Think about how that relationship might unfold in a B2B business. Someone hears about your company from a trusted colleague and asks an AI assistant to compare you with other providers. They visit your website to see whether the recommendation holds up. A few months later, they ask AI another question about the problem they're trying to solve and encounter an article from your company. Eventually, they meet someone from your sales team at an industry event and continue a conversation that's already been shaped by several encounters with your expertise.

The buyer doesn't think of these as separate marketing channels. They're all experiences that contribute to an evolving impression of your company. That's the connective tissue I want a marketing team to understand.

I want the website to reinforce the conversations our salespeople are having, and I want our content to address questions that come up as buyers consider working with us. Now I also want to know what an AI assistant might tell a buyer who asks about our company or the problems we solve. We can't control every AI-generated answer, but we can make our expertise easier to discover and our information clearer and more consistent across the places those systems draw from. That belongs in the same connected marketing conversation as the website and the sales relationship.

When too many tactical specialists are thinking and acting independently, that connected perspective can get lost. We become increasingly sophisticated at managing individual channels without necessarily improving the overall buyer experience.

This matters particularly in relationship-driven B2B businesses, where customers may take months or years to make a purchasing decision. Research from [LinkedIn's B2B Institute on the 95-5 rule](https://business.linkedin.com/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/trends/95-5-rule) reinforces the importance of building familiarity with buyers long before they're ready to purchase. A lean, strategically connected team can be particularly effective because everyone is working from a shared understanding of that relationship.

## AI gives that shared understanding a much greater reach

This is where I think AI creates an extraordinary opportunity for the kind of team I've always preferred.

For years, one of the limitations of lean marketing teams was execution capacity. You could have a brilliant group of people who understood the business and developed excellent strategies, but someone still had to produce the work. And the range of skills required to execute a marketing plan could be considerable.

AI workflows are beginning to change that equation. Imagine taking the shared understanding developed by that small strategic team and building it into a system that supports execution across multiple channels. The system has access to approved positioning and information about the customers we're trying to reach. Its workflows are designed to carry that common knowledge into the marketing work being produced.

I think of it almost as giving the team a shared brain. Rather than having separate specialists continually interpreting the strategy for their own functions, AI-supported workflows can operate from a common foundation. A small team can coordinate a substantial volume of tactical work without adding a person for every channel.

The opportunity extends beyond production, too. With appropriate access to reliable information, AI can connect insights from CRM activity and website behavior with what we're learning through campaign performance. It can bring patterns to our attention that might otherwise remain buried in separate reports.

That changes the conversations we can have as a team. Instead of spending an hour reviewing what happened in five different channels, we can spend that time discussing what we're learning about the buyer relationship and where marketing should focus next.

[McKinsey's 2025 State of AI research](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025) discusses how organizations are redesigning workflows to capture value from AI. That's an important distinction because the opportunity may come from reconsidering how the work happens, rather than simply making existing tasks faster.

Of course, building this kind of system takes thought. An AI workflow is only as useful as the information and direction behind it. It can make mistakes or miss important context, and it needs people who understand the business well enough to recognize those problems.

## Someone still needs to see the whole picture

I've always enjoyed the part of marketing leadership that involves understanding how everything works together. I like seeing how a change in positioning might influence the website or what the sales team is experiencing. I also like figuring out which investments deserve more attention and which ones we can stop making.

That kind of systems thinking becomes even more important when AI is producing a larger share of the tactical work. Someone has to determine what the workflows should accomplish and make sure they have the right information. The outputs need to be evaluated against what we're hearing from customers, and the team needs to recognize when priorities have changed.

There's also judgment involved in knowing when something simply doesn't need to be done. Just because an AI workflow can produce twenty articles doesn't mean the business needs twenty articles.

The [National Institute of Standards and Technology's guidance on generative AI risk management](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence) reinforces the importance of human oversight and ongoing evaluation. For marketing leaders responsible for a company's reputation, those responsibilities deserve serious attention.

I want someone who understands the business providing that oversight. Ideally, it's someone who's close enough to sales leadership to recognize when the market is changing and experienced enough to make informed decisions about how marketing should respond.

## Accountability begins with the business

I've always believed marketing should be accountable for the investment it receives, but I think we've become too accustomed to measuring productivity through marketing activity.

We count what we produce because it's easy to count, and the platforms give us plenty of engagement metrics to report. That information has value, but I also want to understand whether the work is making a meaningful contribution to the business.

For a relationship-driven company, that means understanding how marketing supports the way sales opportunities develop. Sometimes the contribution is measurable through opportunity conversion or sales-cycle performance. Other times, the evidence comes from customer conversations and how effectively our salespeople can communicate the company's value.

I explored this in more detail in [Is There a Marketing Formula That Works for Relationship-Based Sales?](/blog/marketing-formula-relationship-based-sales/). The important thing is to establish measures that reflect the way the company actually wins business.

Accountability also requires a willingness to reconsider an investment when it's no longer serving its purpose. That's often easier to do when marketing isn't organized around maintaining a large permanent production operation. A lean team has more freedom to redirect its attention as the business changes, which is one of the things I've appreciated most about working this way.

## Why this matters more than ever

When I started Win-Win, I wanted to build a business around the way I've preferred to lead marketing throughout my career. I wanted growing B2B companies to have access to experienced marketing leadership without necessarily hiring a full-time CMO. I also wanted that leadership to have the resources to turn strategy into meaningful execution.

AI-supported workflows offer a different way to organize that work, and I find the possibilities genuinely exciting.

I can imagine a small group of capable, curious people who understand the business deeply and enjoy thinking together about how to make it more successful. Their collective intelligence informs a connected marketing system that can produce the work at scale, while experienced human leadership maintains its quality and direction.

For me, that's a much more compelling vision of the AI era than simply producing more content in less time. I've always believed the real value of a marketing team comes from the quality of its thinking and what it does with that thinking. AI gives us an opportunity to put that philosophy into practice with a level of execution capacity that wasn't available before.
