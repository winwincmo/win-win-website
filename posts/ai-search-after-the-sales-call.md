You had a good call. The prospect, who came in from a referral, understood what made your approach different and said they’d bring it up with their team. Now they’re closer to a decision. It’s between you and that other brand they're considering.

If your business runs on relationships, it’s fair to ask what investing in content for AI search would do for you. Your best clients come through people who trust you enough to make an introduction. Your sales team takes it from there. So what can a case study or blog post do when a deal is already in motion?

When a buyer is weighing two or more solutions, AI can make a persuasive case for one of them. Ask which provider can handle a complex transition with the least disruption, and the answer can weigh each option against that specific concern. That’s where your long-form content influences the influencer. A detailed case study can show how you handled a similar transition and what the client’s team had to take on. If AI can find that kind of proof for your competitor and has little to draw on for you, your buyer may have a stronger case for choosing the other firm, even after a great sales call.

Your contact may trust you enough to recommend you. Now they have to explain that recommendation to people who’ve never met you. “I liked their approach” only goes so far when someone asks why they should choose your firm. Give your contact a better answer. Start with the question that could hold up the deal, then publish enough detail for their colleagues to judge your answer for themselves. When they ask AI the same question, it has something specific about your firm to cite.

## Five prompts to see what buyers find between meetings

Pick a real buyer situation and use the language your prospect used on the call. Leave out confidential details. Use an AI tool with web search and start a fresh chat for each prompt. Add this instruction each time: “Use public sources. Link to the source for each important claim and flag anything you can’t verify.”

1. **Can AI explain why you’re a fit?** “We’re a [type of company] looking for [specific outcome]. What does [your brand] do, and how well does its approach fit our situation?” Look for the reason your prospect took the call. Could a colleague who missed it understand the fit from this answer?
2. **Does your difference survive the comparison?** “Compare [your brand] and [other brand] for a company like ours that needs [specific outcome]. Our biggest concern is [constraint]. What differences would matter to us?” Notice whether the comparison reflects what your buyer actually cares about.
3. **Can it find evidence from a similar engagement?** “What public evidence shows [your brand] has delivered [outcome] for a company like ours? Explain what each source actually supports.” Open the links. A service page can confirm you offer something. A case study can show what happened when someone hired you.
4. **What questions will come back to sales?** “Before we recommend [your brand] to our leadership team, what would you want to verify about [specific concern]? What can you confirm from public sources?” Try the objection you hear most often, whether it’s who will do the work or how much time your client’s team will need to commit.
5. **What case could your prospect make internally?** “Write a short memo for our leadership team on whether to choose [your brand] or [other brand] for [specific need]. Explain your recommendation, link to the strongest evidence, and identify any open question that could change it.” Read the memo as if it came from your prospect. Would you recognize the conversation you just had?

Save the prompts and the links the tool used. Pay attention when the same gap shows up across answers. One response can vary with the wording or tool. A pattern gives you something concrete to investigate.

## What’s worth publishing first?

**Start with a deal on your desk.** Ask the person who led the call what the buyer liked and what they still have to take back to their team. Pick one question the buyer is likely to hear internally. That question is your first content brief. If your best prospects keep asking what it takes to switch providers, spell it out on the service page. Describe what your team handles during the transition and where you’ll need the buyer’s involvement.

**Give the buyer something they can forward.** Say you run a commercial insurance brokerage. Your prospect liked that your team starts the renewal process 120 days out. Their CFO, who missed the call, wants to know what that means for their business. A public page that walks through the timeline gives your prospect something concrete to share. A client story can show how that extra time gave a company room to weigh its coverage options before renewal. That’s the kind of detail that carries a sales conversation into the next meeting.

**Put the evidence where buyers can find it.** Give your strongest client stories their own public pages and link to them from the services they support. If a client or partner has already written about the work, make it easy to find that account too. For Google’s AI features, the same search fundamentals apply: publish useful content on pages Google can crawl and index. [Google Search Central’s guidance on AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)

**Use it on the next deal.** Give the page to sales and suggest a moment to send it. A follow-up can be as simple as, “You mentioned your CFO will ask about the switch. Here’s how we handled it for another company with a similar setup.” Once the page is indexed, rerun the prompts and see whether the new evidence shows up. Ask your salesperson what the buyer said after reading it.
