*For growing B2B companies, the answer starts with how you win customers. And in the AI era, the economics of marketing are changing.*

If you're leading a growing B2B company, you've probably wondered whether it's time to hire a marketing agency or bring in more experienced marketing leadership.

Your salespeople are building relationships and bringing in opportunities. Meanwhile, marketing has a growing list of things that need attention. The website could be better. Your sales team needs stronger materials. You'd like to generate more interest from prospective customers, but you're not sure which investments will actually contribute to revenue.

At this point, it makes sense to explore both a marketing agency and fractional CMO services, weighing the advantages of each for your business.

Both can bring valuable expertise to your business. The difference comes down to what you need someone to own.

## What does a marketing agency actually do?

Agencies are typically organized around delivering marketing services. They bring specialized expertise and the capacity to execute work that your internal team may not have the time or skills to manage.

If you already have a clear marketing strategy and know what needs to happen, an agency can be a great investment.

You might need a stronger website or a more effective paid media program. Maybe you're preparing for a major industry event and need creative resources. A good agency can bring experience and execution capacity to those initiatives.

Many agencies also offer strategic advice, and some provide substantial strategic leadership.

But agencies generally work within an agreed scope. Someone inside the business still needs to decide which initiatives deserve funding and how those investments support sales.

That's where things sometimes get complicated.

## What does a fractional CMO bring to the business?

A fractional CMO takes responsibility for marketing leadership without requiring the company to hire a full-time executive.

That means understanding how the business generates revenue and making decisions about how marketing should contribute.

For a relationship-driven B2B company, those decisions often begin with the sales organization.

Consider what we know about B2B buying behavior.

According to [6sense's 2026 B2B Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/buyer-experience-report-2026), **91.4% of buying groups rank their preferred vendors before ever contacting salespeople.** Their initial favorite ultimately wins the business approximately 82% of the time.

That makes the relationship between marketing and sales particularly important.

Your salespeople understand the customers. They know the questions buyers ask and the concerns that can delay decisions. Marketing can extend the value of those relationships by making the company's expertise more accessible throughout the buying journey.

A fractional CMO connects that knowledge to the marketing strategy.

That might mean investing in more useful sales content before launching another advertising campaign. It might mean strengthening the company's positioning so prospective buyers understand why they should consider it.

Sometimes the best investment is improving how the business communicates with customers it already has.

As I explored in [Is There a Marketing Formula That Works for Relationship-Based Sales?](https://winwincmo.com/blog/marketing-formula-relationship-based-sales/), marketing investments should reflect the way a company actually wins business.

The fractional CMO becomes accountable for directing that investment and coordinating the resources needed to deliver it.

And increasingly, those resources may look very different from the marketing teams we've known.

## Why 2027 could change the economics of B2B marketing

One of the developments I'm most interested in watching in 2027 is the difference between teams that use AI individually and organizations that build coordinated, agentic operations.

Today, a salesperson might use AI to research an account. Someone in marketing uses AI to draft content. An agency uses its own AI tools to produce campaign materials.

Each person becomes more productive. That's valuable.

But the organization's collective understanding of the customer may not improve nearly as much.

The information remains scattered across conversations and different systems. When another person starts a project, much of that context has to be gathered again.

And research suggests that individual productivity improvements aren't necessarily translating into comparable organizational results.

In [McKinsey's October 2026 analysis of AI and organizational design](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organization/our-insights/ai-is-changing-work-now-it-has-to-change-the-organization), 80% of respondents reported improved individual productivity from AI, while only 37% reported a positive impact on operating earnings.

Interestingly, nearly three-quarters of the organizations McKinsey classified as AI high performers had substantially redesigned their workflows. Among other organizations, only about one-quarter had done so.

That distinction deserves attention.

What happens when we move beyond making individual employees more efficient and begin rethinking how the entire marketing operation works?

### Imagine a marketing workforce with shared institutional knowledge

Your sales team has developed years of customer relationships. Through those relationships, the company has learned what buyers value and which concerns slow down purchasing decisions. It knows why customers choose your business and what earns their continued trust.

Imagine that knowledge becoming part of a shared, continually maintained information repository that supports an AI workforce.

An agent preparing sales content could draw on that understanding. Another agent supporting a campaign could use the same customer context. A third could analyze performance and identify which messages are resonating with particular buyer groups.

When new information emerges from a sales conversation, it can be reviewed and incorporated into the shared knowledge base.

The next project begins with what the organization already knows.

When someone joins the team or moves into a different role, the company doesn't have to reconstruct so much of its institutional knowledge.

**This continuity may become one of the greatest advantages of agentic marketing operations.**

A connected AI workforce can potentially draw on a broader repository of relevant organizational knowledge than any individual employee can readily recall. It can carry that information across workflows and make it available at the point of execution.

[Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/agents-human-agency-and-the-opportunity-for-every-organization) provides an interesting perspective. Its research found that only 19% of surveyed AI users had both strong individual AI capabilities and an organizational environment prepared to support them.

In other words, organizations still have significant work to do to realize the value of AI beyond individual productivity.

There are important considerations, of course. Shared knowledge must be accurate and current. Customer information requires appropriate access controls. And the agents need clear instructions about what they can do independently and when human review is required.

The technology alone doesn't create an effective operating model.

But the potential is significant.

A coordinated AI workforce could reduce the repeated research and information gathering that consume so much time. It could improve consistency across marketing initiatives and make specialized knowledge available throughout the organization.

I suspect 2027 will give us a much clearer picture of how these operating models compare.

And that raises an interesting question for growing B2B companies: If a smaller marketing team can operate with greater access to institutional knowledge and more coordinated execution, how much permanent marketing infrastructure does the business actually need?

## Where does the fractional CMO fit in an agentic organization?

Someone still needs to understand the business well enough to decide what matters.

AI can extend execution capacity considerably. It can research opportunities and prepare materials. With the right systems, agents can coordinate work that previously required significant manual effort.

But businesses still need experienced leadership to make strategic decisions.

This is especially important as companies increase their investment in AI.

[Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend Survey](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-11-gartner-2026-cmo-spend-survey-finds-cmos-allocate-15-point-3-percent-of-marketing-budgets-to-ai-but-only-30-percent-are-ready-to-scale-ai-capabilities) found that marketing leaders were allocating an average of 15.3% of their budgets to AI initiatives. Yet only 30% reported mature or fully developed AI readiness.

The research primarily represents large enterprises, but the underlying challenge is relevant to smaller companies as well.

Investing in technology is one decision. Building a marketing operation that uses it effectively requires another level of thinking.

For a fractional CMO, that means developing the marketing strategy around the sales motion and establishing priorities. It also means deciding which workflows should be automated and where human expertise is essential.

The CMO provides the judgment and accountability that hold the operation together.

This creates the potential for a leaner marketing model with a different cost structure. A business can invest in senior leadership without adding an entire executive salary to its payroll, while using specialized talent and AI operations for execution.

The goal is to direct more of the marketing budget toward work that actually supports revenue.

## Do you need an agency, a fractional CMO, or both?

I would start by looking at where your business is experiencing the greatest constraint.

If you have strong marketing leadership and a well-defined strategy, but your team lacks the capacity to execute, an agency may be exactly what you need.

If your marketing activity is increasing without a clear connection to sales results, experienced leadership may be the more important investment.

And if you need both leadership and execution, a fractional CMO can coordinate the right resources, including agencies, specialists and AI-supported operations.

These are complementary models when everyone understands their responsibilities.
