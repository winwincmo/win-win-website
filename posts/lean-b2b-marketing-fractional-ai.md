According to [Forrester's September 2026 research on B2B marketing](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/as-ai-adoption-surges-in-b2b-marketing-work-is-changing/), **55% of surveyed organizations are already using AI in production for advertising and media buying**, with most other marketing use cases reporting adoption between 43% and 48%. Those figures aren't a promise of better results, but they do illustrate how quickly the work of marketing is changing. For growth-stage B2B businesses, they also raise a practical question: If AI can take on more of the execution, what kind of team and leadership does a company actually need?

For a long time, growing a B2B marketing function meant adding people. As the business expanded, someone needed to manage the website, develop sales materials, coordinate events, produce content, and keep campaigns moving. Eventually, those responsibilities became too much for one marketing generalist, so the company hired another person or brought in an agency. That approach made sense when there was a fairly direct relationship between the amount of work to be done and the number of people required to do it, but today's changing economics give B2B businesses reason to reconsider the structure of their marketing function.

AI agents and connected workflows are making it possible to organize and carry out parts of marketing that previously demanded considerable hands-on time. For a smaller B2B company, that can mean access to more sophisticated execution capacity without immediately building a large internal department.

The opportunity is compelling, although there's an important condition: Someone still needs to decide what marketing is supposed to accomplish, how it contributes to sales, and whether the work is worth the investment.

## Why do growing B2B companies struggle to scale marketing?

Many B2B businesses grow because they're good at what they do and because their salespeople know how to develop relationships. Referrals lead to introductions, industry events create conversations, and experienced sellers understand which prospects are likely to become customers.

As the company grows, marketing is often asked to support this success without a clear agreement about its role. Leadership wants more visibility, the sales team wants qualified opportunities, and individual departments have ideas about what should happen next. Before long, the marketing calendar is full, but it's difficult to tell which activities are moving the business forward.

Adding employees can relieve some of the pressure, although more capacity doesn't resolve competing priorities or an unclear strategy. A larger team may simply become better equipped to produce work whose business value hasn't been established.

[Forrester's 2026 review of B2B marketing challenges](https://secure.forrester.com/report/b2b-marketings-top-challenges-and-priorities-for-2026/RES197420) describes a similar tension: Expectations for marketing are rising faster than many organizations' readiness to execute and measure the work. The report points to challenges in data, governance, and operating-model capacity even as AI adoption becomes more common.

That's why I think the important question for a growth-stage company is what kind of leadership and operating model will make its existing sales motion more effective. I've written more about this starting point in [What Right-Fit Marketing Actually Means](/blog/right-fit-marketing/), where the emphasis is on shaping marketing around the way a business earns revenue.

## What can AI agents actually do for a B2B marketing team?

The distinction between using an AI tool and building an AI-supported workflow matters.

Asking a chatbot to draft an email or summarize a report can save time, but the employee still has to coordinate the activity around it. A workflow connects activities so information can move through a process with fewer manual handoffs. An AI agent can take on a defined part of that process, working through multiple steps within boundaries set by the business.

Consider a company preparing for an industry trade show. Research into attending accounts could be organized into sales-ready briefs, existing marketing materials could be adapted for different buyer audiences, and follow-up activity could be prepared using the company's approved messaging. With the right systems and review points, much of that preparation can become repeatable rather than starting from scratch for every event.

Or consider the ongoing work of making a business discoverable to prospective buyers. A coordinated workflow might track the questions buyers are asking, identify gaps in the company's published information, assemble research for new content, and prepare drafts for an experienced person to review.

There is good evidence that AI can improve productivity in defined kinds of knowledge work. In a [published study of 5,179 customer-support agents](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), researchers found an average 14% increase in issues resolved per hour after AI assistance was introduced, with larger gains among less-experienced workers. That's a customer-support setting rather than a B2B marketing department, so the figure shouldn't be treated as a forecast for marketing ROI. It does demonstrate how assistance embedded in real work can expand the capacity of a team.

The value comes from reducing the effort required to move useful work from one step to the next. It doesn't mean handing an agent the company's reputation or allowing it to make unsupported claims about products and customers.

## Does a smaller marketing team still need a CMO?

In many cases, this is precisely when experienced leadership becomes more valuable.

When execution becomes easier to produce, it also becomes easier to produce too much of it. Businesses can generate a remarkable volume of content, campaigns, and analysis without becoming any clearer about the customers they want to reach or the reasons those customers should choose them.

A fractional CMO brings executive-level judgment to those decisions without requiring a full-time leadership hire. The responsibility includes evaluating the existing marketing strategy, establishing priorities, aligning marketing with the sales team, and determining which capabilities need to be performed by employees, outside specialists, or AI-supported workflows. If you're weighing an executive leader against another hire, [Do You Need a Fractional CMO or a Bigger Marketing Team?](/blog/fractional-cmo-or-marketing-team/) explores that decision in more detail.

[McKinsey's research on organizations capturing value from AI](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-how-organizations-are-rewiring-to-capture-value) identifies workflow redesign and leadership involvement among the organizational changes associated with meaningful impact. The research doesn't specifically evaluate fractional CMOs, but it reinforces a point that matters here: Technology creates more value when the organization changes how work is directed and managed.

For a business with a relationship-driven sales process, that work has to begin with how revenue is actually generated. A company that wins through referrals and a network of trusted agents or partners may need a very different marketing program from one that depends on high-volume online lead generation.

## How would fractional leadership and AI workflows work together?

I would begin with due diligence before prescribing a new technology stack or restructuring the marketing team. The first job is to understand what the business has already invested in, whether the strategy is sound, how opportunities move through sales, and where marketing work is slowing down.

With that picture in place, the fractional CMO can strengthen the strategy, identify missing foundational assets, and bring a proposed direction to the CEO for approval. An annual marketing budget should distinguish the cost of leadership from program investments such as advertising, trade shows, event marketing, and sales materials.

Only then does it make sense to determine where AI-supported workflows can expand capacity. Some activities may be appropriate for highly repeatable agent-assisted processes. Others will benefit from a specialist, an existing employee, or direct executive involvement.

For example, AI may support initial research into a target industry, while the sales leader validates whether the findings reflect real customer conversations. A workflow may prepare content drafts using approved positioning, while a marketing leader reviews accuracy and judgment before publication. Reporting may become more automated, but deciding how to respond to the results remains a leadership responsibility.

Forrester makes a similar case in its [2026 guidance on AI agents in B2B marketing](https://secure.forrester.com/report/align-ai-agent-use-cases-with-strategic-marketing-initiatives-to-accelerate-the-revenue-engine/RES191658), emphasizing the need to define agent responsibilities, data sources, and connections to strategic priorities before expecting measurable business impact.

The relationship also needs a dependable operating rhythm. Weekly check-ins keep execution moving and decisions visible, while quarterly reviews provide an opportunity to examine whether the marketing investment is supporting the business's growth priorities. For a broader introduction to what that leadership role involves, see [What a Fractional Marketing Leader Can Do for a Growing Business](/blog/what-fractional-leadership-can-do/).

## Is an AI-supported fractional marketing model less expensive?

It can be, although I would be cautious about comparing fees or headcount without examining what the business needs to accomplish.

A full-time marketing executive brings a substantial salary and employment commitment. A larger internal team adds capacity, but also adds management requirements and overhead. Fractional leadership gives a company a way to access executive experience through an agreed scope and time commitment, while AI-supported workflows may reduce the manual effort involved in selected marketing activities.

The savings depend on how well the work is organized, which tools and specialists are required, and how much oversight is necessary. AI systems have costs of their own, and poorly designed workflows can create rework instead of efficiencies.

For that reason, I would measure success in terms of what the business gains: more useful support for sales, clearer priorities, better-managed marketing investments, and improved opportunities to win customers. Lower overhead is meaningful when it accompanies stronger business outcomes.

## What should a growth-stage B2B company do first?

Before hiring another marketing employee or subscribing to another AI platform, examine whether the company has a marketing strategy that the CEO supports and that the sales team recognizes as relevant to the way it wins business.

Then look at the work being done. Which activities require experienced judgment? Which could become repeatable workflows? Where is the marketing team spending time coordinating work rather than moving the business forward?

The opportunity for growth-stage B2B businesses is to build a marketing function with the leadership needed to make sound decisions and the flexibility to execute efficiently. Fractional leadership and AI-supported workflows can be a practical way to do that, when the design begins with the business rather than the tools.
