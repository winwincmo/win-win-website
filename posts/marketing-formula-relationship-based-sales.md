If you run a relationship-based business, you've probably wondered how much you should be spending on marketing.

Maybe you've hired an agency or invested in a new website. Perhaps you've experimented with digital advertising, only to find that the campaigns generated plenty of activity but very little business.

Meanwhile, your best customers continue to come from referrals and relationships your team has cultivated over years.

So what exactly is marketing supposed to do?

It's a fair question. And one that deserves a better answer than spending an arbitrary percentage of revenue.

## Why traditional marketing formulas fall short

Most marketing advice assumes that businesses grow by attracting strangers and converting them into leads. Those leads enter a sales funnel, where marketing continues to nurture them until they're ready to buy.

That model has its place. But it doesn't accurately reflect how many established B2B companies win customers.

In relationship-based industries, buyers often have a sense of who they want to work with long before they're ready to make a decision. Their preferences are shaped by personal recommendations and the reputation a company has built over time.

Trust matters enormously in these decisions, particularly when the services are complex or the stakes are high.

Research from [LinkedIn's B2B Institute](https://business.linkedin.com/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/trends/95-5-rule) reinforces the importance of building familiarity long before someone is ready to buy. Its 95-5 rule suggests that, in many B2B categories, only a small percentage of potential buyers are actively in the market at any given time.

That's worth considering when so much marketing investment is focused on generating immediate leads.

A marketing program built entirely around lead generation can overlook the relationships responsible for generating revenue. Worse, it can consume a significant budget without making the business any more effective at winning customers.

## Start with how your business actually wins customers

Before deciding what marketing should cost, I would want to understand how your company generates business today.

Where do your best opportunities come from? Why do customers choose you over a competitor? And what typically happens between the first introduction and a signed agreement?

The answers reveal something a standard marketing budget formula cannot. They show where marketing has an opportunity to make a meaningful contribution.

Consider a company whose team has spent decades building relationships within a specialized industry. They know their customers well and have developed a reputation for solving difficult problems.

But the company's website barely communicates that expertise.

A prospective customer receives a glowing recommendation, visits the website, and finds generic messaging that could describe almost any competitor.

That's a marketing problem worth solving.

Another company might have an excellent reputation but limited reach beyond its existing network. In that case, marketing could create opportunities by making its expertise more discoverable to buyers who don't yet know the company.

The investment should reflect the opportunity.

## Marketing should make relationships more valuable

Your relationships have something incredibly valuable at their core: trust.

That trust has been earned over years of showing up for customers and following through on commitments. Marketing should build on that foundation and extend its value beyond the people who already know your business.

That might mean making your company's expertise easier to find or giving your team a clearer way to communicate what makes your company different.

A thoughtful case study could be exactly what someone needs after a promising conversation. An article that addresses a common industry challenge might give a referral partner something worth sharing.

Even a simple customer newsletter can create opportunities to reconnect.

These activities may not produce impressive lead-generation reports. But they can influence whether a conversation turns into an opportunity.

There's research to support the continued importance of personal interaction. A [2026 Gartner survey of B2B buyers](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-20-gartner-survey-finds-sixty-nine-percent-of-b-two-b-buyers-turn-to-sales-reps-to-validate-ai-generated-insights) found that 69% preferred to validate AI-generated insights with sales representatives. Buyers may be comfortable researching independently, but they still turn to people for reassurance when making important decisions.

That creates an interesting opportunity for marketing.

Your digital presence can establish confidence before a conversation begins. Your team can build on that confidence through the relationships they already have.

## So how much should you spend?

This is where I think many business owners become understandably frustrated.

They've been told to allocate a certain percentage of revenue to marketing or that they need to invest more heavily in digital channels.

But nobody has clearly explained what those investments should accomplish.

I prefer to work backward from the business opportunity.

Suppose your company has a healthy pipeline but struggles to close opportunities. Before spending money on more leads, I'd want to understand what's preventing those opportunities from converting.

Perhaps your positioning isn't clear enough. Or competitors may be doing a better job of demonstrating their expertise.

Marketing investment should address those issues first.

The same logic applies to staffing.

Does your company really need a full-time marketing director and an internal team? Or would experienced strategic leadership supported by specialized resources be a better use of your budget?

That's a question I explore further in [Do You Need a Fractional CMO or a Bigger Marketing Team?](/blog/fractional-cmo-or-marketing-team/).

For many growing businesses, the challenge is figuring out which marketing capabilities are necessary at their current stage of growth. And which ones aren't.

## Marketing waste often starts with the wrong question

When someone asks me how much they should spend on marketing, I wonder whether the more important question is what they're spending money on today.

It's surprisingly easy for marketing expenses to accumulate without anyone evaluating their contribution.

An agency gets hired to manage social media. Someone recommends paid advertising. A new software subscription promises better lead generation.

Each investment may seem reasonable on its own.

But who's looking at the whole picture? Who's asking whether these activities support the way the business actually wins customers?

Without that oversight, marketing can become a collection of expenses rather than a coordinated investment in growth.

This is one reason I believe lean marketing models deserve more consideration. Experienced leadership can establish priorities while specialized resources execute the work.

I discuss that approach in [How a Leaner Marketing Team Can Fuel Growth](/blog/lean-b2b-marketing-fractional-ai/).

The objective is to spend where the business has the greatest opportunity to benefit.

## Measure marketing against the way you sell

If your business grows primarily through referrals, why would you judge marketing success exclusively by website leads?

Of course, leads matter. But they may represent only a small part of marketing's contribution.

I'd also want to understand whether marketing is making it easier for your team to build relationships and win business.

Are prospects arriving at the first meeting with a clearer understanding of your company? Are existing customers introducing you to new opportunities? And are you winning more of the business you pursue?

These questions connect marketing performance to commercial outcomes.

Some contributions will be easier to measure than others. You can track referral sources and opportunity conversion rates. You can also ask your team which materials they use and whether those resources are improving conversations.

Marketing deserves accountability. That accountability should reflect how your business makes money.

## Is there a formula?

Yes, although it probably isn't the one you've been given.

**Understand how your business wins customers. Identify where marketing can make that process more effective. Invest in those opportunities and measure the results.**

The formula will look different from one business to another because the sales dynamics are different.

A company entering a new market may need to invest heavily in awareness. An established company with a strong referral network may benefit more from strengthening its positioning and supporting its team.

Both can be sound marketing strategies.

What matters is that the investment makes sense for the business.

After more than 25 years working in marketing strategy, I've become increasingly convinced that the most valuable marketing question isn't how much we can do.

It's how much of what we're doing actually matters.

And for relationship-based businesses, the answer usually begins with understanding how trust translates into business.
